What are Quantile Treatment Effects?

The Average Treatment Effect (ATE) or Average Treatment Effect on the Treated (ATT) summarizes the causal effect of a treatment by a single number: the mean difference in outcomes between treated and untreated units. This is often a natural quantity, but it can miss important heterogeneity. If a job training program substantially raises earnings at the bottom of the distribution while having little effect at the top, the ATE obscures this. If a minimum wage increase compresses the wage distribution, an average effect conceals the compression.

The Quantile Treatment Effect at level τ is QTE(τ) = QY(1)(τ) − QY(0)(τ), where QY(d)(τ) is the τ-th quantile of the potential outcome distribution under treatment status d. Mapping QTE(τ) across τ ∈ (0, 1) traces how the treatment shifts the entire outcome distribution. The Quantile Treatment Effect on the Treated (QTT) conditions on the treated group.

This vignette demonstrates unc_qte(), the qte package’s estimator for cross-sectional settings (no panel data required). For DiD-based estimators and staggered treatment adoption, see vignette("panel-estimators") and vignette("staggered-adoption").

library(qte)
library(ggplot2)
set.seed(42)
data(lalonde)
xf <- ~ age + I(age^2) + education + black + hispanic + married + nodegree

Random assignment

Under random assignment, no covariate adjustment is needed: the unconditional quantiles of the treated and control outcome distributions identify the QTE directly. unc_qte() implements the semiparametrically efficient estimator of Firpo (2007). We use the experimental Lalonde data (lalonde.exp), where treatment was randomly assigned.

res_exp <- unc_qte(
  yname  = "re78",
  dname  = "treat",
  data   = lalonde.exp,
  target = "qte",
  probs  = seq(0.1, 0.9, 0.1),
  biters = 100
)
summary(res_exp)

Earnings are zero for 31% of this sample, so unc_qte() warns that the outcome looks discrete, and summary() repeats the note. Quantiles in the mass point are zero in both groups, so the QTE there is zero by construction; interpret the low quantiles with care.

autoplot(res_exp, ylab = "QTE (earnings, 1978)")

The point estimates are zero at the lowest quantiles, inside the mass point, and then increase with τ, from about $900 at the 30th percentile to about $2,900 at the 90th. With 445 participants, though, the uniform band covers zero at every quantile, even though the ATE of about $1,800 is statistically significant: a band that holds simultaneously over nine quantiles is much wider than a single interval.

Unconfoundedness

When treatment is not randomly assigned, the unconfoundedness assumption (conditional independence of potential outcomes given covariates) provides identification (Firpo 2007). We use the observational Lalonde data (lalonde.psid), which combines the treated group from the experiment with a comparison group from the PSID.

unc_qte() supports three estimation methods:

  • "ipw" — inverse probability weighting (propensity score re-weighting)
  • "reg" — outcome regression (conditional quantile regression)
  • "aipw" — augmented IPW (doubly robust; recommended)
xf <- ~ age + I(age^2) + education + black + hispanic + married + nodegree

res_psid <- unc_qte(
  yname      = "re78",
  dname      = "treat",
  data       = lalonde.psid,
  xformula   = xf,
  est_method = "aipw",
  target     = "qte",
  probs      = seq(0.1, 0.9, 0.1),
  biters     = 100
)
summary(res_psid)

These estimates are large and negative, far from the experimental benchmark above. The QTE is about the whole population, which here is mostly PSID households with much higher earnings than the program’s participants, and these covariates (which leave out earnings before the program) do not make the two groups comparable (LaLonde 1986; Dehejia and Wahba 1999).

QTT under unconfoundedness

Setting target = "qtt" estimates the Quantile Treatment Effect on the Treated — the distributional effect for the subpopulation that actually received treatment.

res_qtt <- unc_qte(
  yname      = "re78",
  dname      = "treat",
  data       = lalonde.psid,
  xformula   = xf,
  est_method = "aipw",
  target     = "qtt",
  probs      = seq(0.1, 0.9, 0.1),
  biters     = 100
)
summary(res_qtt)
#>

The QTT only needs a counterfactual for the participants, so it is much smaller in magnitude than the QTE, but it is still negative and grows with τ, and the band excludes zero from the 40th percentile up. It remains well below the experimental estimates, a reminder that unconfoundedness given these covariates is a strong assumption for this comparison group.

Reading the output

summary() prints the overall ATT, the QTE/QTT at each quantile, and standard errors with confidence intervals. autoplot() returns a ggplot object so you can add layers:

autoplot(res_qtt) +
  ggplot2::labs(title = "QTT — Lalonde (observational)",
                subtitle = "AIPW with pre-treatment covariates")

By default autoplot() shows a uniform confidence band (cband = TRUE), which provides simultaneous coverage over all quantile levels — a stronger guarantee than pointwise intervals. Pass cband = FALSE for pointwise intervals instead.

Standard errors are computed via the empirical bootstrap. The biters argument controls the number of iterations (default 100). Parallel computation is available via the cl argument.
